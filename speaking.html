Speaker Bureau

Jerry Peterson

Author & Speaker · Creator of the Z Scale

Jerry Peterson is the Founder of RightSide Inc. and an Enterprise Agile and AI Coach with more than 30 years of Fortune 50 practitioner experience. He is the creator of the Z Scale and the author of Raising the Dead, and he speaks globally on workforce engagement, AI transformation, and human performance systems.

His work reframes disengagement not as a personal failing but as a system problem — one that leaders have the power to solve. Drawing on the lineage of Deming, Goldratt, Daniel Pink, and Gene Kim, the Z Scale gives leaders a way to see exactly where their people and teams stand, and a path to bring them back to life.

His talks pair hard systems thinking with a deeply human message: being beaten down by the system is not a character flaw, and it is always reversible.

Signature Keynotes

The four that carry the whole argument. Best place to start.

Signature

The Human Constraint: The Bottleneck Every Leader Misses

In 1984, Eliyahu Goldratt proved that the output of any system is determined entirely by its constraint — fix anything but the bottleneck and you get zero gain. For forty years leaders have hunted that constraint in their processes and technology and missed the most common one of all: a disengaged person staffing it. Jerry Peterson calls it the Human Constraint — and shows why no amount of tooling releases it, and what actually does: giving people autonomy, mastery, and purpose.

Signature

The Human System: You Don't Have a People Problem

Take two identical employees. Put one in a system where decisions move fast, mistakes are safe, and people see their impact; put the other in a maze of approvals, blame, and invisible work. Six months later one is a Zealot and one is a Zombie — nothing changed but the system. Building on Deming's finding that 94% of failures belong to the system, this talk is about the inversion every leader misses: we manage the people and ignore the system they're trapped inside.

Signature

In Between: Why Capable Leaders Freeze on the Bridge to the Future

Behind every leader is what they were trained to do — plan, control, inspect, approve. In front of them is the work they now have to lead — cognitive, creative, fast-moving, AI-augmented. They can see the future and talk the language of it, then Monday arrives and they ask for a business case and say "why wasn't I consulted?" That's the Leadership Translation Gap. This talk hands leaders the four practical translations that carry them across: from controlling people to designing conditions; activity to outcomes; permission to proximity; busyness to protected focus.

Signature

The Zealot Dividend: The Real ROI of Waking Up Your Workforce

There's a number that should keep every CEO, CFO, and board member awake at night: the fortune your organization loses every year to disengagement — money you're not being robbed of, but giving away. This talk makes the financial case for the opposite move: what happens when an organization stops manufacturing Zombies and starts cultivating Zealots. The return is massive, compounding, and measurable. If you've ever suspected your biggest untapped asset is already on your payroll, this is the math behind that instinct.

For the C-Suite & Finance

The cost of doing nothing — and the recoverable upside — in the language of the P&L.

The $8.9 Trillion Heist Hiding Inside Every Organization

Every year the global economy loses trillions to disengagement — the largest heist in human history, and there are no robbers. Jerry Peterson traces how organizations quietly give that money away through the systems they build and the management habits they refuse to let go of, and what it takes to stop the theft. (Recorded at $8.9 trillion; Gallup's 2026 figure is $10 trillion — the heist is growing.)

The $8.9 Trillion Problem Every CFO Is Ignoring

For the finance leaders in the room: disengagement shows up as lost yield inside your own organization. Jerry Peterson makes the numbers case — the yield loss, Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, and the HP LaserJet proof that you can raise output without adding a single hire. The engagement crisis, in the language of the P&L. (Recorded at $8.9 trillion; now $10 trillion and still climbing.)

The Latent Productivity Proof: Double Your Output Without Hiring Anyone

Your organization already has the capacity to lift throughput 20–30% — and in your critical bottlenecks, to double it — without hiring, buying software, or touching the org chart. With only 20% of the global workforce fully engaged, most companies get the full output of a fraction of the people they pay for, and roughly 94% of that suppression traces to management-owned systems. Apply the Theory of Constraints to the human bottleneck everyone ignores and the whole value stream accelerates.

Management's $10 Trillion Alibi: The Excuse Executives Hide Behind

If our management practices are good enough to defend, why does disengagement cost the global economy more than the GDP of nearly every country on earth? When confronted with the figure, leaders look outward and blame the workforce. This talk proposes a harder explanation: it isn't happening to management — it's being created by it. The trillions are the alibi executives hide behind to avoid owning the environment they built.

The Information Age Switch: Why We Still Manage Like It's 1910

We have a $10 trillion problem — $2 trillion of it in the US. We already know the cause (Gallup finds 70% of the variance in engagement traces to the manager) and the cure (Information Age environment architecture). So why do we still run organizations on an operating system designed for 1910? Because we are paying our leaders to maintain the problem. Put the cost of your Zombies on the P&L, reward leaders for fixing it, and the heist ends.

The $700 Billion Opportunity: The Growth Hiding on Your Payroll

Every executive is under pressure to find growth — M&A, new markets, AI, automation. But the largest organic growth opportunity in the American economy needs no capital expenditure, no acquisition, and no new technology. With only about a third of the US workforce fully engaged, the rest are operating below potential. This talk lays out the recovery math — and why released capacity becomes a competitive moat no rival can copy by writing a check.

For Leaders & Managers

The daily habits that manufacture Zombies — and how to build the environment that creates Zealots.

Are You the Problem? The Leadership Question No One Answers Out Loud

In thirty years, Jerry Peterson has never had a leader answer it out loud: are you the cause of the disengagement on your team? Drawing on Deming's 94%, he introduces "the Vote" — the idea that every leadership decision moves people toward engagement or away from it — and an honest five-question report card for finding out where you stand.

Deming's Indictment: Stop Blaming Your People

When leaders see the cost of disengagement, their first instinct is to blame the workforce — people don't want to work anymore. They're wrong. In 1986, W. Edwards Deming gave us the formulation every leader needs to memorize: 94% of failures are failures of the system, not the person. This is the indictment that follows: you cannot mandate engagement or buy it with a bonus. You have to build the environment that creates it.

The Environment Architect: Why Your Team Won't Take Ownership

"I just want my team to take ownership." It's the universal executive lament — they see a lack of initiative and conclude the problem is a deficit of character. The uncomfortable truth: your team hasn't taken ownership of the work because you haven't yet taken ownership of the environment they do it in. The leader's job isn't to be the hero; it's to build the environment where heroes can emerge.

Why Your Best People Quietly Check Out — and How to Bring Them Back

Gallup measures the same staggering number every year: most of the workforce is disengaged. Jerry Peterson explains how organizations turn motivated people into Zombies who do the bare minimum — Deming's 94%, and the story of "Alex" — and the changes that convert them back into engaged Zealots who bring their full effort.

Work Is Where People Look for Meaning — Most Systems Crush It

For a growing share of people, work is a primary source of purpose — and a system that treats them as cogs isn't just a management failure, it's a moral one. Jerry Peterson reframes leadership as stewardship of the human spirit, and argues the moral choice and the economic choice are the same one.

The Folly of Rewarding A While Hoping for B

In 1975, Steven Kerr named the mistake organizations still make every day: rewarding one behavior while hoping for another. Jerry Peterson shows how misaligned incentives quietly manufacture disengagement — "people are coin-operated; they listen to the compensation plan, not the poster" — and how to build a scoreboard that rewards the behavior you actually want.

The Surveillance Trap: How Watching Your People Kills Their Work

When work went remote, organizations panicked and bought the keystroke loggers, the screenshot tools, the dashboards — and built the most expensive Zombie factory in history. Every surveillance tool sends one message: I don't trust you. It uses the most powerful technology of our time to enforce the most bankrupt management idea of the last century — a straight line back to Taylor's stopwatch. Why monitoring erodes the exact performance it's trying to protect, and what to measure instead.

For AI & Transformation

Why new technology and new methodologies fail on old systems — and what has to change first.

Why Most AI Deployments Fail — The Problem-First Imperative

Deploying AI on top of a broken system doesn't fix the system — it builds a faster version of the same dysfunction. Jerry Peterson makes the case for a problem-first discipline: auditing where your people's time actually goes before you automate anything, so AI delivers a real return instead of an expensive one.

The 1910 Decision

The layoffs we're seeing because of AI repeat the decisions made in 1910, when factories chose machines over craftsmen. It's a mistake today because our work is now mental, not physical. Industrial Age thinking treats people as liabilities to minimize; Information Age thinking treats them as the assets that build the company's future. AI should free people from soul-sucking work to be more creative — not replace them.

The Transformation Illusion: Why "Agile Our Way" Is the Most Expensive Sentence in Business

Organizations pour billions into transformation and most of it fails — not because the methodology was wrong, but because leadership adopts the vocabulary and rituals of change while quietly protecting the Industrial Age environment the change was meant to replace. You can't install a new operating system on a machine that refuses to reboot. Why the pattern persists, and the problem-first discipline that breaks it.

What the Mongols Knew: Why New Technology Is Never Enough

Three thinkers, three principles, and the largest contiguous empire in history. Goldratt: technology is necessary but never sufficient — you have to change the rules. Marquet: turn the worst submarine into the best by pushing authority to the people with the most information. And promote capability over tenure. Put them together and you get the operating system behind history's most improbable empire — and a mirror for your own organization.

For Change Agents & Talent

For the people trying to fix it from inside — and the way we hire the problem in the first place.

The Productivity You Already Have (And Keep Suppressing)

Organizations chase more capacity — more hires, more tools — while the capacity they already have sits trapped in their own systems. Using the HP LaserJet story and the "Visibility Vacuum," Jerry Peterson shows how a simple bottleneck audit releases the productivity already sitting in your people. A talk about doing dramatically more with the team you already have.

The Change Agent's Survival Guide: Why Being Right Gets You Fired

A confession: over thirty years Jerry Peterson has been fired, removed, or quietly sidelined from more engagements than he can count — not for being wrong, but for being right and assuming the organization wanted the problem solved. That's the Zealot's Dilemma: the qualities that make you valuable get you branded a threat by the organization's immune system. The survival guide he wishes he'd had — how to get real change through without becoming a casualty.

You're Hiring the Wrong Things: The Question That Manufactures Zombies

Recruiting runs on one flawed question: "Can you do this job?" But capability doesn't predict engagement. Hire someone qualified but not passionate and you'll get a competent professional — for a while — and then a Zombie. This talk makes the case that we hire for capability when we should hire for what actually predicts engagement, and that the potential you want is often already in your office, waiting for permission to care again.
